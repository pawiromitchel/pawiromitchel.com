Exposing local services with Cloudflare Tunnel

No port forwarding, no dynamic DNS, no home IP on the internet. How to put a local service on a real domain with cloudflared, and the catches worth knowing first.

  • infrastructure
  • devops
  • networking
  • homelab
  • security

Putting something from your own machine on the internet used to mean the same three chores: forward ports 80 and 443 on the router, set up dynamic DNS because the ISP keeps changing your IP, and then accept that your home IP is now out there getting port-scanned.

Cloudflare Tunnel skips all of that. You run a small daemon, cloudflared, next to your service. It opens an outbound connection to Cloudflare, and requests for your hostname come back down that connection. Nothing listens on your router. Cashly runs behind one of these.

How it works

Because your side opens the connection, this also works behind CGNAT, double NAT and networks where you couldn't forward a port even if you wanted to.

The quick version: no account, no domain

If you just need to show someone a local build or point a Stripe or GitHub webhook at your laptop, a quick tunnel is enough.

A permanent tunnel on your own domain

Things to know before you rely on it

  • Cloudflare sees your traffic. TLS ends at their edge, and they open a new connection to you. That's how the WAF and caching work, but it means your requests pass through Cloudflare unencrypted on their side.
  • Protect admin tools with Access. A tunnel makes a service reachable, not private. For things like Portainer, add a Cloudflare Access policy so visitors have to log in with Google, GitHub or a one-time email code before they ever reach your app.
  • Uploads are capped. The free plan limits request bodies to 100 MB, which you'll notice if you tunnel a file-sharing or photo app.
  • It's a dependency. If Cloudflare has a bad day, so does your service. That's fine for a homelab and worth a thought for anything else.
