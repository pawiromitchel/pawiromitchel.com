Building Cashly, my self-hosted finance tracker

I stopped using budget apps and built a Go daemon that reads bank alert emails and tracks fiat and crypto in one place.

Mitchel Pawirodinomo

Technical Operations Engineer at QuickNode

Most ways of tracking personal finances end up in one of two places.

The first is the cloud aggregator. You give Plaid, Yodlee or MX your bank login, and it works until their scraper breaks and your balances freeze for three weeks. And if you bank outside the US or Europe, your bank usually isn't in the dropdown at all. Tracking staking rewards or DeFi positions next to that? Not happening.

The second is the spreadsheet. You build an 18-tab Google Sheet, and it works right up until you skip entering receipts for two weekends and the cash flow numbers stop meaning anything.

What I wanted was something that runs on my own hardware, picks up transactions without me typing them in, and shows bank accounts and crypto side by side. I also wanted to be able to say "I paid $35 cash for groceries" instead of filling in a form. That became Cashly.

How it's put together

There are two pieces: a Go daemon that does the real work (scheduled jobs, ledger maths, the API) and a Next.js web app in front of it. The web app sits behind a Cloudflare Tunnel and a PIN login, and talks to the Go API with an internal token.

The Excel workbook stays in sync in both directions, so a spreadsheet is still there when it's the quicker tool.

Bank sync without handing over passwords

Instead of logging into banks, Cashly reads their notification emails. Most banks and fintech apps send an email the moment a card is charged, a transfer lands or cash comes out of an ATM. The Go daemon checks the mailbox over IMAP every two minutes and turns those emails into transactions.

The catch with email is that you can get the same message twice. A dropped IMAP connection means a re-fetch, and a re-fetch must not mean a double charge in the ledger. So every alert gets a fingerprint before it's written:

When the bank includes a Message-ID header, that alone makes the hash unique. When it doesn't, the timestamp, the amount in cents and the subject still do. If the hash is already in the transactions table, the email is skipped.

Following the money, not just totals

A pie chart tells you that 30% went to food. It doesn't tell you that money came in as a USD invoice, went through Payoneer, got converted to local currency and then paid rent. I wanted to see that path, so Cashly draws cash flow as a Sankey diagram:

For that to work, a transfer between accounts is stored as more than a single row:

  • the main movement (out of one account, into another)
  • the exchange rate, if it crosses currencies
  • fees (gas, wire fees) booked as their own expense

The Go daemon builds a graph from those rows, and the frontend renders it. That's also where burn rate, runway and savings rate come from.

Prices are fetched on a schedule and cached in SQLite, so loading the portfolio page doesn't call any external API. Valuations come back in under 2 ms, and rate limits stop being a problem.

An assistant that can undo

Cash is the hardest thing to track. If recording a coffee means opening an app and tapping through five screens, you'll stop doing it within two weeks. So Cashly has an assistant, built on Gemini, that I can just tell:

"Paid $35 cash for groceries at the supermarket yesterday evening"

It works out the merchant, category and account, and records the transaction:

Letting a language model write to your ledger is only okay if you can take it back. Every change the assistant makes is logged with a snapshot of the row as it was before:

When it gets an amount or a category wrong, "undo that" restores the snapshot in one transaction. No poking around in SQLite by hand.

Market indicators

I DCA into a few assets, so Cashly also calculates some long-term indicators for BTC, ETH, SOL and the S&P 500:

  • Pi Cycle top and bottom: the 111-day moving average against twice the 350-day, and the 471-day average × 0.745. Historically these have lined up with cycle extremes.
  • EMA ribbons: the 20, 50 and 200-week EMAs, as a quick read on whether we're in a bull or bear trend.

None of this is advice. It's a dashboard I check before my monthly rebalance.

Keeping it private

  • All the data is in a single SQLite file.
  • The Go daemon and the web app run as two Docker containers behind the Cloudflare Tunnel.
  • There are no analytics or tracking scripts.

If you're building something similar, or want to compare notes on parsing bank emails, get in touch. The project lives at cashlyfinance.com.
